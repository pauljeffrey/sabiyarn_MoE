# Small-Scale Farming and Crop Rotation in West Africa

Crop rotation is the practice of growing different types of crops in the same field across different seasons, rather than planting the same crop year after year. In much of West Africa, smallholder farmers have long rotated staples like maize, cassava, yam, and groundnut with legumes such as cowpea or soybean, often without formal training, simply because experience showed it kept the soil productive for longer.

The main benefit of rotation is soil fertility. Cereal crops like maize and millet draw heavily on nitrogen in the soil, while legumes such as cowpea, groundnut, and soybean have root nodules that host bacteria capable of fixing nitrogen from the air back into the soil. Planting a legume after a season of cereal cultivation naturally restores some of the nitrogen the cereal removed, reducing the amount of fertilizer needed in the following season.

Rotation also helps break the life cycles of pests and diseases that build up when the same crop is planted repeatedly in one field. Many soil-borne pathogens and insect pests specialize in a single crop or crop family; when that crop is absent for a season, the pest population in the soil declines sharply. This is one reason continuous cassava or continuous maize cultivation on the same plot tends to show declining yields and rising pest pressure over time compared to a rotated system.

A common smallholder rotation cycle in the savanna zones of West Africa follows a cereal-legume-tuber pattern: for example, maize or sorghum in the first season, cowpea or groundnut in the second, and yam or cassava in the third, before returning to cereal. In the forest and derived-savanna zones further south, farmers more often rotate maize with cassava and cocoyam, sometimes intercropping legumes between rows in the same season rather than strictly alternating season by season.

Fallowing, or leaving land uncultivated for a period, was traditionally combined with rotation to let soil fertility recover further, but population growth and pressure on farmland have shortened fallow periods considerably in many areas. This has made deliberate crop rotation and the use of nitrogen-fixing legumes even more important for maintaining yields where long fallow is no longer practical.

Rotation planning also has to account for market demand and household food needs, not soil science alone. A farmer choosing what to plant after a maize season is weighing the soil benefits of a legume against the cash value of a tuber crop like yam, and often settles on a mixed strategy: rotating the main plot for soil health while keeping a smaller plot in a high-value crop each season regardless of rotation.

Agricultural extension programs across the region increasingly promote structured rotation schedules alongside other practices like minimum tillage and the use of certified seed, since combining these practices tends to produce larger yield gains than any single practice on its own. Farmer field schools, where groups of farmers test a rotation plan together over several seasons and compare results, have been an effective way of spreading these practices beyond farmers who received formal extension visits.
