# Malaria Prevention and Treatment

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites, which spread to people through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. These mosquitoes are most active between dusk and dawn, which is why bites most often happen at night or in the early evening. Once a parasite enters the bloodstream, it travels to the liver, multiplies, and then re-enters the blood, where it infects red blood cells and causes the symptoms associated with the illness.

The most common early symptoms of malaria are fever, chills, headache, and general body weakness. These can appear anywhere from seven days to a few weeks after an infective mosquito bite. Some people also experience nausea, vomiting, muscle pain, and sweating as the fever breaks. Because these symptoms overlap with many other common illnesses, a blood test is the only reliable way to confirm malaria rather than relying on symptoms alone.

If left untreated, malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum, the most dangerous species in West Africa, can progress quickly to severe illness. Severe malaria may involve organ failure, severe anemia, difficulty breathing, or cerebral malaria, in which the parasite affects the brain and can cause confusion, seizures, or coma. Children under five and pregnant women face the highest risk of severe complications and death, which is why health workers prioritize rapid testing and treatment for these groups.

Sleeping under a long-lasting insecticide-treated net (LLIN) is one of the most effective and affordable ways to prevent mosquito bites at night. Nets should be checked regularly for holes and re-treated or replaced as recommended, since damaged nets lose much of their protective value. Indoor residual spraying, in which insecticide is applied to the inside walls of a home, is another widely used community-level prevention method, particularly in areas with high transmission.

Reducing mosquito breeding sites around the home also lowers risk. Anopheles mosquitoes breed in still water, so clearing stagnant water from containers, gutters, old tires, and blocked drains after rainfall can meaningfully cut down local mosquito populations. Wearing long sleeves and trousers in the evening, and using approved mosquito repellents, adds another layer of protection, especially for people who work or travel outdoors after sunset.

For pregnant women in moderate-to-high transmission areas, health authorities recommend intermittent preventive treatment during antenatal care visits, in addition to sleeping under a treated net. This reduces the risk of malaria-related complications for both mother and baby, including low birth weight.

When malaria is confirmed by a rapid diagnostic test or microscopy, treatment with an artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) is the standard first-line treatment recommended in most of West Africa. It is important to complete the full course exactly as prescribed, even if symptoms improve after a day or two, because stopping early can allow the parasite to survive and contributes to drug resistance over time. Anyone with high fever who cannot access prompt testing and treatment, especially a child, should be taken to the nearest health facility without delay, since severe malaria can worsen within hours.

Community health workers play an important role in many rural areas, offering rapid diagnostic testing and first-line treatment closer to where people live, which reduces delays caused by distance to a hospital or clinic. Public health campaigns that combine free or subsidized net distribution, indoor spraying, community health worker outreach, and public education have been shown to significantly reduce malaria cases and deaths in West African countries over the past two decades.
